# Find the hard part

Spend most of the words on the one idea that unlocks the rest.

## Why

Most of a subject is easy once one or two ideas click. Those ideas are where readers get stuck, and where they fake understanding by repeating the words. Spreading effort evenly across every component gives the hard idea the same three lines as the easy ones.

## How

- Ask: which idea, once understood, makes the rest obvious? That is where to spend the effort.
- Look for the usual kinds of hard:
  - **Tacit:** experts do it without saying it ("of course this runs on another thread").
  - **Ritual:** a step people copy without knowing why.
  - **Jargon:** an everyday word with a special meaning here ("commit", "state", "cost").
  - **Interaction:** each part is simple, but how they behave together is not.
- For the interaction kind: you can introduce the parts one at a time, but you still have to show them running together.
- Give the hard idea its own diagram or step, its own example, and a check question.

## Example

In the bookshop's card payment, the boxes are simple. The hard part is that "approved" does not mean the money is taken. The API only authorizes the charge, and a worker captures it later, after checkout has returned. Give that split its own picture and spend less time on the boxes.

## Watch out

- Knowing the parts is not the same as knowing the system. A reader can name every class and still not predict what happens.
- The hard part for the reader is often not the hard part for the explainer. See [01-know-the-reader.md](01-know-the-reader.md).

Based on: threshold concepts (Meyer and Land); troublesome knowledge (Perkins); element interactivity (Sweller).
