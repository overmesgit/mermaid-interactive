# Start from a question

Open with the question, the failure, or the surprise that the idea answers.

## Why

An answer means little to someone who never had the question. Curiosity comes from a gap the reader can see: they know enough to notice something is missing. An explanation that starts with the problem gives every later detail a job. An explanation that starts with the solution makes the reader hold details without knowing what they are for.

## How

- Lead with the failure or the question in one or two sentences: "Why can an order be paid twice?"
- Make the gap small and specific. "How does the system work?" is too wide to pull anyone along.
- Show the case that the obvious approach gets wrong before showing the approach that works.
- Let the reader attempt it briefly if they are able to. Even a failed attempt makes the answer stick better.
- Close the gap. Come back to the opening question at the end and answer it directly.

## Example

A PR explanation that opens with "Customers were charged twice when the payment page timed out. Here is why, and here is the fix" reads very differently from one that opens with "This PR adds an idempotency key table."

## Watch out

- A question the reader cannot follow yet is not a gap, just noise. Give them enough to see it.
- Do not drag out a puzzle when the reader needs the answer now (an incident, a quick review).

Based on: Loewenstein's information-gap theory of curiosity; Kapur's productive failure; Schwartz and Bransford's "a time for telling".
