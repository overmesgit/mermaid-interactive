# Multimedia learning (Richard Mayer)

How people learn from words and pictures. Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML) uses three assumptions:

1. **Dual channels.** Separate processing for verbal and pictorial material. Early processing follows sensory modality (eyes vs ears); later processing follows presentation mode (verbal vs pictorial). Draws on Paivio's dual coding and on Baddeley's working memory.
2. **Limited capacity.** Each channel can handle only a limited amount at once. Draws on Sweller's cognitive load theory.
3. **Active processing.** Meaningful learning means selecting relevant material, organizing it, and integrating it with prior knowledge. Mayer's SOI model: select, organize, integrate.

Paivio (1986) is the dual-coding source: verbal and imaginal codes, and the claim that the two together support memory and understanding better than one alone. Sweller is the capacity source: extra material that is not required for the goal consumes the same limited working memory as the material that is required.

## Three design goals

From Mayer's later summary (the UNH chapter below):

- **Reduce extraneous processing.** Processing that does not serve the goal. Coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity.
- **Manage essential processing.** The material itself is more than the learner can hold at once. Segmenting, pre-training, modality.
- **Foster generative processing.** Capacity is available but the learner is not working to make sense of it. Personalization, voice, embodiment.

## Principles named in a review of the theory

A review of twelve principles (Clark & Mayer's line of work; the *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning* abstract linked below) reports these, with median effect sizes from the comparisons in that review:

| Principle | Claim in brief | Reported median d |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multimedia | Words and graphics beat words alone | 1.67 |
| Coherence | Fewer extraneous words and sounds | 0.70 |
| Signaling | Cues to the essential material | 0.46 |
| Redundancy | Narration plus graphic beats narration plus graphic plus the same text on screen | 0.87 |
| Spatial contiguity | Corresponding words and pictures near each other | 0.79 |
| Temporal contiguity | Corresponding words and pictures at the same time | 1.30 |
| Segmenting | Learner-paced chunks | 0.70 |
| Pre-training | Names and characteristics of the parts before the process | 0.46 |
| Modality | Spoken words with a graphic, rather than printed words with the same graphic | 0.72 |
| Personalization | Conversational wording | 0.79 |
| Voice | A human voice rather than a machine voice | 0.74 |
| Embodiment | On-screen agent drawing or gesture | 0.36 |

The same abstract says some principles are stronger for low-knowledge learners than for high-knowledge learners. That boundary matters if a skill always adds a picture, a story voice, or a pre-training glossary.

An earlier statement of five of the principles (Mayer & Moreno, 2002): multiple representation (words and pictures rather than words alone), contiguity, coherence, modality, and redundancy.

## What this does not settle

These principles were measured on instructional messages (for example, how a pump or lightning works), mostly with narration and animation. A chat explanation is a different medium. Modality and voice assume spoken audio. Embodiment assumes an on-screen agent. The transferable part for a text skill is the rest: pair a necessary picture with the words, put the words on the picture, cut decoration, name parts before the process, and segment.

## When the material itself is the problem

Pre-training and segmenting manage essential processing. Sweller's term for why some material is hard in itself is **element interactivity**: elements that must be held in mind at once to make sense interact, and elements that can be learned one after another do not. Pollock, Chandler, and Sweller (2002) tested an **isolated-interacting elements** sequence: first present the elements as separate pieces, without their interactions, then present the full version. For some learners (the abstract says "certain groups") this beat presenting the full version twice. From the repository record and search excerpts; the paper was not opened.

This is the cognitive-load version of the "underlying game" problem in [threshold-concepts.md](threshold-concepts.md): the pieces can be taught alone, but the understanding is in how they interact, and that part still has to be taught as a whole.

- Pollock, E., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2002). Assimilating complex information. *Learning and Instruction*, 12(1), 61–86. Record: [UOW repository](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/145)
