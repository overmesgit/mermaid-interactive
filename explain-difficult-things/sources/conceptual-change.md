# Conceptual change (Posner, Strike, Hewson, and Gertzog)

What to do when the person already holds an idea that competes with the one being explained. The other notes mostly assume the learner is missing something. This one is for when the learner has something, and it is wrong, or right in a different frame.

Ausubel's anchoring idea does not require the anchor to be correct. Taber makes that point directly: an alternative framework is a perfectly good anchor, and the learner then makes a different sense of the material from the one intended. See [taber-prior-knowledge.md](taber-prior-knowledge.md).

## Four conditions for accommodation (1982, p. 214)

Posner et al. separate assimilation (new material fits current concepts) from accommodation (central concepts have to be replaced or reorganized). For accommodation they give four conditions "common to most cases":

1. **Dissatisfaction** with the existing conception. People are "unlikely to make major changes in their concepts until they believe that less radical changes will not work." The learner needs "a store of unsolved puzzles or anomalies."
2. **Intelligible.** The learner can grasp how experience could be structured by the new concept well enough to explore it. They name analogies and metaphors as what gives a new concept its first meaning.
3. **Initially plausible.** The new concept looks able to solve the problems its predecessor could not, and is consistent with other knowledge.
4. **Fruitful.** It suggests further problems it could open up.

The order matters for an explanation. An explanation that is intelligible but never made the old idea fail asks for a change the learner has no reason to make.

## What makes an idea plausible (p. 218)

At least five ways, in the paper's list:

1. Consistent with the learner's metaphysical beliefs and epistemological commitments.
2. Consistent with other theories or knowledge.
3. Consistent with past experience.
4. The learner can find or create images for it that match their sense of what the world could be like.
5. It solves problems the learner already knows about (resolves anomalies).

The fourth is the representational one. A picture is not only for intelligibility. It can be what makes the new idea believable.

## Teaching implications they state (pp. 225–226)

From their list for "teaching aimed at accommodation":

- Create cognitive conflict: problems and demonstrations the current idea cannot handle.
- Spend time diagnosing errors and the "defensive moves" learners use to keep the old idea.
- Represent content in several modes (verbal, mathematical, concrete-practical, pictorial) and help the learner translate between them. This is the same task Ainsworth calls translation. See [ainsworth-deft.md](ainsworth-deft.md).
- Track the change, for example with a clinical interview.

Two teacher roles beyond "clarifier of ideas and presenter of information": a Socratic adversary to the conception (not the person), and a model of scientific thinking that demands consistency between belief and evidence. The paper says plainly that whether these changes would work had not been tested.

## Refutation text

A text form built on the same idea: state the common misconception, say that it is wrong, then give the correct explanation and why it works better. Tippett's (2010) review of two decades of science-education studies is cited for the claim that refutation texts beat ordinary expository texts at correcting misconceptions. Later papers are reported to be more skeptical about the size of that effect. Neither the review nor the skeptical papers were opened here; this paragraph is from search excerpts.

For a chat explanation this is the cheapest version of conceptual change: name the likely wrong idea out loud before explaining the right one, instead of explaining the right one and hoping the wrong one goes away.

## Where it is weak

The 1982 paper is a theory from philosophy of science plus interviews about special relativity. Later critics (not read here) argued it is too rational and leaves out motivation and the learner's affect. Taber's own caution applies: some alternative frameworks are tenacious, and gains may be transitory. A single explanation may produce the right answer today and the old idea in a month.
