# Bruner's three modes, and CRA

Two linked ideas about the order of representations: start with something the learner can act on or picture, and only then use the formal symbol.

## Bruner: enactive, iconic, symbolic

Jerome Bruner described three ways of representing experience:

- **Enactive.** By action. The learner does the thing.
- **Iconic.** By image. A picture or diagram stands in for the action or object.
- **Symbolic.** By language or other symbols, including notation.

CRA teaching sequences cite this (often via Bruner & Kenney, 1965) as the reason for moving from objects, to drawings, to symbols. Bruner also proposed the **spiral curriculum**: the same ideas return later at a higher level of sophistication, rather than being taught once in full. Elaboration theory describes itself as a more specified relative of that spiral, plus Ausubel's general-to-specific order. See [elaboration-theory.md](elaboration-theory.md).

## CRA: concrete, representational, abstract

A mathematics sequence (Mercer & Miller, 1992, as described in the sources below):

1. **Concrete.** The learner uses physical objects.
2. **Representational.** The learner draws pictures that stand in for those objects (dots, tallies, circles).
3. **Abstract.** Numbers and symbols only, after the earlier levels.

The KU page's rationale: the drawing is the bridge from a tangible case to the symbol, and the sequence is meant to join the procedure ("how") with the concept ("why" and "what"). Mastery at one level before the next is part of that description.

Variants in the research include virtual manipulatives in place of physical ones (VRA), and adding an advance organizer or a mnemonic for steps. A review excerpt said about half the studies used an organizer, and that this was not a significant moderator. The KU page itself returned an error when fetched; the description here is from the search excerpt of that page and from the SAGE review abstract.

CRA is a strong fit when the hard part is a formal system (notation, a formula, a type signature). It is a weak fit when there is no sensible concrete case, or when the person already works in the symbols and a return to counters would add load. The related caution is the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga, cited in [examples.md](examples.md)), which Mayer's review also reflects when it says some principles are stronger for low-knowledge learners: extra support that helps novices can get in the way of people who already have the schema.
