# Threshold concepts and troublesome knowledge (Meyer and Land; Perkins)

A way to say which ideas in a subject are the difficult ones, and why they are difficult. The other notes are about how to explain. This one is about where to spend the effort.

## Threshold concept

From Land, Cousin, Meyer, and Davies (2005), the third paper in the series:

- "A threshold concept represents a transformed way of understanding, or interpreting, or viewing something without which the learner cannot progress" (p. 53).
- It is **transformative** (a significant shift in how the subject is seen), **irreversible** (unlikely to be forgotten, or unlearned only through considerable effort), and **integrative** (exposing the previously hidden interrelatedness of something) (p. 53). It also brings a shift in how the learner sees themselves, and an extended use of discourse.
- "Concepts that bind a subject together" (p. 54). A student who has not yet got one "has little option but to attempt to learn new ideas in a more fragmented fashion."

Economics examples from the paper: opportunity cost, price and value, equilibrium. An accountancy lecturer's example: depreciation.

## Troublesome knowledge (p. 58)

Knowledge may be troublesome "because it has become ritualised, or inert, because it is conceptually difficult or alien, because it is tacit and perhaps requires awareness of an 'underlying game' imperceptible to the student, or because of the discourse that has to be acquired" (citing Meyer & Land, 2003, and Perkins, 1999).

Short glosses, from secondary summaries, not from Perkins directly:

| Kind | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Ritual | Used by rote, as a routine, without its meaning |
| Inert | Known, but not called on when it applies |
| Conceptually difficult | Complex or abstract in itself |
| Alien | From a perspective the learner does not share |
| Tacit | Mainly personal and implicit; shared inside a community but rarely said |
| Language | The discipline uses an everyday word with its own meaning ("cost" in accounting) |

## The underlying game (pp. 55–56)

Land et al. use programming as one of their examples. Students "may grasp the concepts of class, objects, tables, arrays, and recursion, but they may not appreciate the threshold conception, the underlying game, of the interaction of all these elements in a process of ever-increasing complexity." The parts are understood. The way they behave together is not.

This is the case a parts-first explanation misses. Elaboration theory's epitome (a small whole that already runs) is one answer. See [elaboration-theory.md](elaboration-theory.md).

## Liminality and mimicry (p. 55)

A learner can be stuck "in-between," oscillating between the earlier understanding and the full one. One outcome is **mimicry**: a partial or superficial version of the concept, often in the right words. For an explanation, this is the warning that the person repeating the term back does not show they have crossed.

## What the authors suggest (pp. 57–58)

- Use threshold concepts to decide where in a course to put the effort: the "jewels in the curriculum" and the "stuck places."
- Engagement: ask the learner to explain the concept, represent it in new ways, apply it to new situations, and connect it to their life, rather than recall it in the form it was given.
- Listen first. "It is difficult for teachers, experienced and expert within the discipline, who long since travelled similar ground in their own disciplinary excursions, to gaze backwards across thresholds" (p. 58). See [curse-of-knowledge.md](curse-of-knowledge.md).
