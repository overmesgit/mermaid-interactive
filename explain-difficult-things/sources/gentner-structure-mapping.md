# Structure-mapping (Dedre Gentner)

A theory of how an analogy is interpreted. The useful claim for explanations: an analogy carries **relations between objects**, not the objects' surface attributes, and the relations that travel are the ones tied together by higher-order relations such as cause.

## Claims, from Gentner (1983)

Interpretation maps knowledge from a base domain (the familiar one) into a target domain (the new one). Two mapping principles:

1. Relations between objects are mapped, rather than attributes of objects.
2. Which relations are mapped is set by systematicity: relations that participate in higher-order relations (a causal chain, a constraint) are preferred over isolated facts.

The rules are described as depending on the structure of the representation, not on the specific content of the domains. The same paper distinguishes analogy from literal similarity, abstraction, and other comparisons.

A later review (Gentner & Smith) treats mapping as the core process, and lists further learning effects of analogy: inference projection (bringing a fact across), schema abstraction, difference detection, and re-representation. Comparing two analogs, rather than studying one, is reported as helping people transfer a relational schema (Gick & Holyoak, 1983, is the classic citation in that review).

## Overlap with elaboration theory

Reigeluth and Stein's analogy component says to relate the new idea to a familiar one and to show where the analogy breaks down (1983, p. 360). Structure-mapping is a stricter account of what "relate" should mean: align the causal or constraint structure, and the breakdown is a relation that does not map.

## A teaching procedure: Teaching With Analogies (Glynn)

Glynn's TWA model turns the same ideas into steps. The wording varies across the secondary sources; the six, as they are usually given:

1. Introduce the target concept.
2. Cue retrieval of the familiar analog.
3. Identify the relevant features of each.
4. Map the similarities.
5. Indicate where the analogy breaks down.
6. Draw conclusions about the target.

Step 5 is the one structure-mapping says to keep and the one most often dropped. A study of physics analogies, reported in a search excerpt, found most lacked a step identifying limits, which raises the risk of new misconceptions. A broken mapping that is not named becomes an alternative framework. See [conceptual-change.md](conceptual-change.md).
