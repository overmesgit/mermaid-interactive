# Providing examples

Guides for choosing, pairing, and retiring examples. They overlap. A worked solution, a concrete case of an abstract idea, and a non-example are different jobs. Nothing here is a rule for the skill yet.

The research this note uses was read from the pages linked below, or from search excerpts where a page is marked as not fully read.

## What the example is for

- **Worked example.** A problem, the solution steps, and the answer, for someone who does not yet have the skill. Studying it beats solving from scratch at the start, because search for a solution uses the capacity needed to see the principle (Sweller; summarized in Renkl, 2014).
- **Concrete example.** A specific case of an abstract idea. Abstract material is harder than concrete material (Paivio, Walsh, & Bons, 1994, as cited by Weinstein, Madan, & Sumeracki, 2018).
- **Boundary pair.** An example and a near non-example, so the learner can see what the idea is not.

## One example: how to build it

Atkinson, Derry, Renkl, and Wortham (2000) group design into features inside one example and features of the set.

**Inside one example**

- Integrate the parts. A diagram and its explanation in one place. Split attention across a figure and a distant caption uses capacity the example needed (Tarmizi & Sweller, 1988).
- Mark the subgoal structure. Label steps or isolate them visually. Catrambone's work, cited there and again in Renkl (2014), finds that a salient subgoal leads learners to explain what the step accomplished, which helps them reassemble steps for a new problem.
- A complete solution is not always better. Leaving a gap can force an inference. Renkl's later qualification: do not add that demand when the material is already too heavy.

**The set**

- At least two examples of the same problem type. Reed and Bolstad (1991), as cited in the 2000 review: one example is often not enough to induce a usable idea; a second, especially a harder one, helps transfer. "At least add a second example" is their basic rule.
- Vary the cover story within a type, and reuse the same cover stories across types, so a surface detail is not a reliable cue to the deep structure (Quilici & Mayer, 1996, via Renkl, 2014).
- Put each example next to a matched problem that is similar but not identical. Alternating example, problem, example, problem beat a block of examples followed by a block of problems (Trafton & Reiser, 1993).

**What the learner does with it**

Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann, and Glaser (1989): the example works when the learner explains the rationale of the steps, not when they only read them. Renkl (1997) found two successful styles. Principle-based explainers state the rule the step instantiates. Anticipative reasoners predict the next step and check it. Most learners in that study were passive. Prompts and short training can induce the explanation (Renkl, Stark, Gruber, & Mandl, 1998). If the learner cannot produce a correct explanation, an instructional explanation helps; if they can, they should do it themselves (Schworm & Renkl, 2006; Wittwer & Renkl, 2010, average added-explanation effect d = .16, and not in every condition).

## Renkl (2014): nine principles

Renkl's instructionally oriented theory of example-based learning joins worked examples, observational learning, and analogical reasoning. His prescriptive table, shortened. The third column is his own qualification.

| Principle | Learning is better if… | Qualification he states |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-explanation and comparison | learners compare examples and explain the rationale | no effect when the task already overloads them |
| Explanation-help | they get an instructional explanation | mainly conceptual knowledge, mathematics, or explanations given by default rather than on request |
| Model-observer similarity | the model seems like them | which features of similarity matter is unclear |
| Example-set | the set makes the critical aspect stand out | no effect unless comparison is prompted |
| Easy-mapping | different sources (text, picture) are easy to relate | no rule for which device to use: integration, color, modality |
| Meaningful building blocks | the sense of a single step is salient | established mainly for mathematical procedures |
| Studying errors | correct and incorrect worked examples are both shown | low prior knowledge needs extra support to study the errors |
| Imagery | after study, the learner imagines the steps | no effect without enough knowledge to imagine them |
| Interleaving by fading | worked steps are removed as learning proceeds | established mainly for mathematical procedures |

Fading, from the same paper: a complete example, then the same kind of example with one step missing, then more blanks, until only the problem remains. Feedback follows each attempt. Adaptive fading, dropping a step after the learner has explained that kind of step, is described as especially effective (Salden, Aleven, Renkl, & Schwonke, 2009). Forward versus backward fading was not settled by Renkl, Atkinson, Maier, and Staley (2002).

Examples are for the early and middle phases: encoding the principle, then seeing it applied. Once the goal is speed and automaticity, more examples can get in the way (expertise reversal; Kalyuga, 2007). Renkl notes a general lack of classroom studies over long periods.

## Several examples: what to vary

**Variation theory** (Marton & Tsui, 2004). A critical aspect is discerned only when it varies against something that does not. Four patterns, in Marton and Tsui's wording as restated by Lo and Marton and by later summaries:

1. **Contrast.** A quality is not discerned without a mutually exclusive alternative. What it is, beside what it is not.
2. **Separation.** One dimension varies while the others stay fixed, so that dimension can be seen on its own.
3. **Generalization.** The critical value stays fixed while the context changes, so the idea is not tied to one case.
4. **Fusion.** Several critical aspects vary together, after they have been separated, so the whole can be seen.

A suggested order in the secondary summaries: contrast and separation first, then fusion, with generalization to carry the discernment into new contexts. The Cambridge Mathematics espresso notes that researchers do not agree on the exact sequence, and that a well-built set is not enough: attention has to be drawn to the pattern. Marton and Pang's conjecture, tested in a small game study, is that a new meaning comes from difference against a background of sameness, not from sameness against a background of difference.

**Engelmann and Carnine**, *Theory of Instruction* (1982; reprinted 1991). Five juxtaposition principles, quoted from a secondary restatement of the book (the blog below). The book itself was not opened.

1. **Wording.** The same wording, or nearly the same, on every item, so language changes do not compete with the example.
2. **Setup.** The first examples and non-examples share as many irrelevant features as possible.
3. **Difference.** Juxtapose items that differ only in the critical feature, and mark them as different. A non-example that also changes color, position, and shape leaves the learner guessing which change mattered.
4. **Sameness.** Juxtapose examples that differ as much as possible and still count, and mark them as the same, so the range is visible.
5. **Testing.** New examples and non-examples, in random order, so a correct answer is not a pattern in the sequence (yes, no, yes, no).

**Contrasting cases** (Schwartz & Bransford, 1998). Analyzing simplified cases that differ in specific ways prepares someone to understand a later explanation. They call the result a "time for telling." In three classroom studies, generating the distinctions and then hearing the lecture or reading the text produced better predictions a week later than reading the distinctions, summarizing a text, or analyzing the cases twice with no explanation. The cases do not replace the explanation. Twice through the cases without the telling was worse.

Gentner's comparison work is the same neighborhood: two analogs, aligned on relations, beat one. See [gentner-structure-mapping.md](gentner-structure-mapping.md).

## Concrete examples, and where they mislead

Weinstein, Madan, and Sumeracki (2018), in their review of six learning strategies:

- If the concrete example and the abstract instruction disagree, people follow the example (LeFevre & Dixon, 1986).
- An interesting detail that is not the point can crowd out the point (Harp & Mayer, 1998).
- Novices remember the cover story. Experts extract the structure (Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser, 1981).
- Two examples with different surface details transfer better than one (Gick & Holyoak, 1983). How many is enough is not settled.
- An example that is not concrete enough is hard to understand. One that is too concrete can block generalization to the abstract idea.

A practical restatement from a course guide that cites that paper: state the idea, give the example, then point back at which part of the example is the idea. Offer more than one, with different surface details. The link from example to idea should be said, not left implicit.

## What this does not settle for a chat explanation

Most of the worked-example evidence is from well-structured procedures: probability, geometry, programming. Fading and subgoal labels are clearest there. A conceptual explanation may need the boundary pair and the second, different-looking example more than a faded solution. Renkl's own qualifications are the caution: do not add a comparison prompt, an error case, or a missing step when the person is already at capacity, and do not keep giving full examples after they can do the thing.
