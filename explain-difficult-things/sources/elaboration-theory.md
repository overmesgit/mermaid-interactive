# Elaboration theory (Charles Reigeluth)

Macro-level sequencing. Start with a simple version of the whole subject or task, then add detail or difficulty one step at a time, and keep tying the new piece back to the whole.

Reigeluth compares it to a zoom lens: a wide view of the major parts and how they relate, zoom in on one part, zoom back out, repeat. It is meant for medium to complex cognitive or psychomotor content whose topics depend on each other. It does not cover mainly affective content. It decides the order of a course, not how a single idea is taught inside one lesson. That micro level was left to Merrill's Component Display Theory.

This is not elaborative rehearsal in memory research, and not the Elaboration Likelihood Model of persuasion.

## Epitome

The first lesson. It teaches a few fundamental ideas, or the simplest version of the whole task that is still representative, at a level where the learner can apply them.

Reigeluth and Stein (1983) say the first ideas "epitomize rather than summarize the ideas that follow" (p. 343). A summary states many ideas at a superficial, abstract, memorization level. An epitome teaches a very small number of ideas at a concrete, meaningful, application level. "To epitomize is not to lightly preview all of the course content" (p. 343). Epitomising means identifying very general or very simple ideas, but not abstract ones (p. 346).

For a task, the 1999 chapter: "Epitomizing is the process of identifying the simplest version of the task that is still fairly representative of the whole task" (p. 444).

Their economics example: the epitome is the law of supply and demand, taught with examples and practice until the learner can use it. A syllabus, a topic list, or a same-level summary is not an epitome. An Ausubel advance organizer is a close relative and still not an epitome: the organizer is a general bridge, the epitome is a usable simplest whole.

After each later lesson, an expanded epitome reviews what came before and fits the new ideas into the whole (1983, pp. 364–365).

## Three sequences (1999)

- **Conceptual.** Many related concepts. Broadest, most inclusive concepts first, then narrower ones. Narrower is not the same as harder: children usually learn what a dog is before they learn what a mammal is (p. 438).
- **Theoretical.** Many related principles. The most basic, general principles first, then more detailed ones.
- **Simplifying conditions.** A task of at least moderate complexity. The simplest real version first, then harder versions. Versions that each remove a different simplifying condition can be learned in any order (pp. 448–449).

A course can use more than one. Topical sequencing and spiral sequencing are options, not rules (pp. 432–433). The spiral idea comes from Bruner; see [bruner-cra.md](bruner-cra.md).

## Simplifying Conditions Method

Begins with the simplest version of the task that is still fairly representative of the task as a whole, then teaches progressively more complex versions (1999, p. 442). Each version is a complete, real-world performance. A hierarchical sequence does not teach a complete real-world task until the end (p. 443). Prerequisites stay, but they are taught inside the lesson that needs them, not as a front-loaded glossary. "The hierarchical approach is necessary but not sufficient" (p. 443).

Epitome criteria (p. 444): a whole version of the task, simple, usually real-world, and fairly representative (typical or common). Each elaboration is another whole version, slightly more complex, equally authentic or more so, and equally or slightly less representative. The very simplest version is often not representative of the whole task (p. 446).

## Seven strategy components (Reigeluth & Stein, 1983, p. 342)

1. **Elaborative sequence.** Simple-to-complex. Later lessons add detail or complexity and stay connected to the whole.
2. **Learning-prerequisite sequence.** An idea is not presented until its prerequisites have been taught, inside the lesson that needs them (pp. 357–358). Draws on Gagné's learning hierarchies.
3. **Summarizer.** Systematic review: a concise statement of each idea, a reference example, and a few self-test items (p. 358).
4. **Synthesizer.** Relates and integrates ideas, with examples, and grows as the instruction grows (p. 359).
5. **Analogy.** Relates new ideas to familiar ones from outside the subject, and shows where the analogy breaks down (p. 360).
6. **Cognitive-strategy activator.** Prompts a thinking strategy, such as imaging the process or predicting an outcome (pp. 361–362).
7. **Learner control.** Informed choice of what to elaborate next, because the learner has already seen a whole version (pp. 362–363).
