# ICAP: what the learner does (Chi and Wylie)

A classification of what the learner is doing while they learn, by overt behavior. It is the missing counterpart to the representation notes: those say what to show, this one says what to ask the person to do with it.

## Four modes (2014)

The hypothesis, I > C > A > P: "the *Interactive* mode of engagement achieves the greatest level of learning, greater than the *Constructive* mode, which is greater than the *Active* mode, which in turn is greater than the *Passive* mode" (p. 219).

- **Passive.** "Being oriented toward and receiving information from the instructional materials without overtly doing anything else related to learning" (p. 221). Reading an explanation.
- **Active.** "Some form of overt motoric action or physical manipulation is undertaken" (p. 221). Highlighting, copying, rotating a model, clicking through.
- **Constructive.** The learner generates "additional externalized outputs or products beyond what was provided in the learning materials" (p. 222). The output has to contain new ideas. A self-explanation that repeats the text is only active. Drawing a diagram the example did not have is constructive; copying the one it had is active.
- **Interactive.** Dialogue where "(a) both partners' utterances must be primarily constructive, and (b) a sufficient degree of turn taking must occur" (p. 223). The partner can be a computer agent, if it responds in a content-relevant way.

Constructive activities they list (p. 222): concept maps, notes in one's own words, asking questions, posing problems, comparing and contrasting cases, integrating text and diagrams.

## The point for an explanation in chat

A chat is a dialogue, so it can be interactive in Chi and Wylie's sense, but only if the person's turns are constructive. "Does that make sense?" invites a yes, which is at best active. The paper's example of a better tutor prompt is a fill-in request, "OK, and it goes from the atrium to the [pause]?", because it needs more than yes or no (p. 222).

Prompts that would move a reader from passive to constructive:

- Predict the next step, or the output, before it is shown.
- Say which part of the example is the idea. See [examples.md](examples.md), which has the same finding from the self-explanation side (Chi et al., 1989; Renkl, 1997).
- Compare two cases and name the difference. See the contrasting-cases section of [examples.md](examples.md).
- Draw or change the diagram.

## Caveats the authors state

The modes are defined by overt behavior. A person reading quietly may be covertly constructive, and the framework would score them passive (p. 221). The authors give a section to caveats and factors that can override the ordering. Active, constructive, and interactive still depend on what the person already knows: Renkl's qualification in [examples.md](examples.md), that prompts do not help a learner already at capacity, applies here too.

## A reason to ask: the illusion of explanatory depth

Rozenblit and Keil (2002) had people rate how well they understood everyday devices (a cell phone, a crossbow, a sewing machine), then write a step-by-step causal explanation, then rate again. Ratings dropped after the attempt. The illusion was stronger for explanatory knowledge than for facts, procedures, or narratives. This is from the abstract as returned in search; the paper was not opened.

For an explanation skill, this is the reason "do you understand?" is a poor check, for the person and for the explainer alike. Asking for a short causal account, or a prediction, is the test that the feeling of understanding does not pass on its own.
