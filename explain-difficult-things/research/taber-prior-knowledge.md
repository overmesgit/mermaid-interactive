# Mismatch with assumed prior knowledge (Keith Taber)

A typology for when a lesson assumes knowledge the learner does not have in the form the teacher expects. The point is diagnosis: name the kind of mismatch, then choose the repair. "The key idea is the need to arrange the content presented to match the learners' own cognitive structures" (manuscript p. 12).

Read from the author's manuscript. Page numbers below are the manuscript's, not the journal's.

## The starting point

Taber builds on Ausubel. Meaningful learning needs two things: the learner holds relevant prior knowledge, and the learner recognizes that it is relevant ("makes the connection"). Then the step Ausubel left open: the principle "does not specify that the learner's prior knowledge has to be accurate" (p. 4). An alternative framework is a perfectly good anchor. The learner then makes sense of the material, but "a different sense to that intended" (p. 5).

## The typology (pp. 9–12)

Two classes. **Null** impediments: the learner cannot make sense of the material in terms of existing ideas. **Substantive** impediments: the learner interprets the material through existing but alternative ideas.

| Type | Class | Nature, in Taber's table | Action, in Taber's table |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deficiency | Null | No relevant material held in existing cognitive structure | Remedial teaching of prerequisite learning (if available), or restructuring of material with bridging analogies |
| Fragmentation | Null | Learner does not see relevance of material held in cognitive structure to presented material | Make connections between existing knowledge and new material explicit |
| Ontological | Substantive | Presented material inconsistent with intuitive ideas about the world | Make learner's ideas explicit, and challenge them where appropriate |
| Pedagogic | Substantive | Presented material inconsistent with ideas deriving from prior teaching | For the learner, treat as ontological; for the future, re-think the teaching: order and manner of presentation |

For fragmentation, Taber says the fix may be "no more than including a wider range of examples (perhaps including those from more familiar situations)," or analogies between disparate topics (p. 10). ## Two observations worth keeping

**The learner knows about one kind and not the other.** "A pupil suffering from a null learning impediment is likely to be aware that she does not understand the teacher. However, the outcome of a substantive learning impediment is to understand differently" (p. 10). A person who says "I don't get it" is probably in the null class. A person who says "got it" may be in the substantive class. Asking them to predict or explain is how to tell. See [icap-engagement.md](icap-engagement.md).

**Teaching creates some of the wrong ideas.** Taber's example is the "octet framework" in chemistry (pp. 8–9). Pupils are taught, accurately, that some electron arrangements are stable. Why reactions happen is left out as too hard. Pupils fill the gap themselves: atoms react "so that" they can get full shells. They build whole schemes on it, and later chemistry contradicts it. Taber's point is that the order and the simplification of the teaching supplied the material, and that a change in teaching could avoid it, for example by pointing out that reactants already have stable structures too.

For an explanation skill this is the warning about simplifications: a simplification that leaves a "why" unanswered invites the learner to answer it, and the invented answer can outlast the explanation.

## Sources of alternative ideas (p. 7)

Everyday experience (the impetus idea that motion needs a continuing force), folklore and media, and the teaching itself. Some alternative ideas are about things far from experience, such as how atoms interact, so they cannot have come from everyday life.

## Limits the author states

The scheme is for teachers who already have motivated pupils, a workable environment, and well-sequenced lessons, and still do not get the learning they expected. It "over-simplifies": real knowledge is "multifaceted, highly responsive to idiosyncratic aspects of context, and somewhat fluid" (p. 13). The line between ontological and pedagogic is "not intended to be absolute, but rather of utility to teachers" (p. 11). Gains against a tenacious framework may be transitory (p. 5). Concept mapping is suggested to see how integrated the learner's knowledge is (citing White & Gunstone, 1992).

## Why it is in this folder

Ausubel says to find out what the learner already knows. Taber says "already knows" fails in different ways, and the repair differs: supply the missing piece, connect the pieces they have, or challenge the piece that is wrong. The last one is its own topic; see [conceptual-change.md](conceptual-change.md).
