# Meaningful verbal learning (David Ausubel)

How people learn a large amount of meaningful material from an explanation, by attaching it to ideas they already have. The line often quoted from Ausubel, Novak, and Hanesian (1978, p. 163), via a later commentary: the most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows; ascertain this and teach accordingly.

## Subsumption

New material is related to relevant ideas already in the learner's cognitive structure, in substance, not as a verbatim string. Forgetting, in this account, is details being absorbed into the larger idea and losing a separate identity.

Instructional Design.org's summary of the theory limits it to reception learning: an explanation the learner receives. Ausubel separates that from rote learning (no subsumption) and from discovery learning (the learner has to find the information by problem solving).

## Advance organizers

Introductory material, more abstract, general, and inclusive than the lesson, chosen because it can explain, integrate, and interrelate what follows (Ausubel, 1963, p. 81, as quoted on Instructional Design.org).

An organizer is not an overview or a summary. Those sit at the same level of abstraction as the lesson and only emphasize key ideas. The organizer is a bridge between related ideas the learner already has and the new material.

If the learner has few relevant ideas, the organizer needs to be more expository: more of the framework is supplied, in familiar words. That fifth characteristic is from the Reading Horizons commentary on Ausubel et al. (1978), linked below. The same commentary lists these characteristics of an organizer:

1. More inclusive, abstract, and general than the material it precedes.
2. Takes into account relevant ideas the learners already have.
3. Shows the relationship between those ideas and the new ones.
4. More expository when relevant prior ideas are scarce, using familiar terminology.

## Two principles (Instructional Design.org)

1. Present the most general ideas of a subject first, then progressively differentiate them into detail and specificity.
2. Integrate new material with what was already presented, by comparison and cross-reference.

Ausubel (1963) uses Boyd's pathology textbook as progressive differentiation: organized by general processes such as inflammation and degeneration, rather than by organ systems in isolation.

## Relation to the epitome

Elaboration theory treats the advance organizer as a relative of the epitome, not the same thing. The organizer is a general bridge presented before the lesson. The epitome is a small concrete whole the learner can apply. See [elaboration-theory.md](elaboration-theory.md).
